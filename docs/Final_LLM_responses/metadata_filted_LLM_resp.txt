## Nationalism in Europe – Chapter Overview

### Direct Answer
The chapter explains how 19th‑century nationalism reshaped Europe, replacing multi‑national dynastic empires with modern nation‑states. It traces the roots of nationalism to the French Revolution, where sovereignty shifted from an absolute monarch to the people, and shows how this idea spread across the continent.

### What It Means
- **Nationalism**: A political ideology that emphasizes the interests, culture, and identity of a particular nation, often seeking self‑governance.
- **Nation‑state**: A sovereign political entity where the boundaries of a nation (people sharing common culture, language, or history) align with the boundaries of a state (centralised government with territorial control).

### How It Works (Key Steps in the Chapter)

1. **Historical Context**  
   - Europe in the 18th‑19th centuries was dominated by multi‑national dynastic empires (e.g., Habsburg, Ottoman, Russian).
   - These empires governed diverse peoples under a single monarch.

2. **Catalyst: The French Revolution (1789)**  
   - France transitioned from an absolute monarchy to a republic.
   - Sovereignty moved from the king to the citizens.
   - The revolution declared that the nation is constituted by the people, who shape its destiny.

3. **Spread of Nationalist Ideas**  
   - The French example inspired other European peoples to seek similar self‑determination.
   - Nationalist movements began to form around shared language, culture, and history.

4. **Emergence of the Nation‑State**  
   - Nationalism led to the creation of states whose borders matched national identities.
   - Centralised power exercised sovereign control over clearly defined territories.
   - Dynastic empires gradually dissolved or restructured into nation‑states.

5. **Visual Representation**  
   - Fig. 1 (by Frédéric Sorrieu, 1848) illustrates the dream of worldwide democratic and social republics, symbolising the ideal of nation‑state cooperation.

### Example from the Chapter
- **French Revolution**: The shift of sovereignty from the monarchy to the French citizens is presented as the first clear expression of nationalism. It demonstrates how a people can collectively define and govern their nation.

### Important Points to Remember
- **Nationalism’s Core Idea**: The nation is defined by its people, not by a monarch.
- **Transformation of Europe**: The 19th‑century shift from dynastic empires to nation‑states reshaped political boundaries and governance.
- **Centralised Sovereignty**: Modern nation‑states possess a centralised government that exercises sovereign control over a defined territory.
- **Historical Catalyst**: The French Revolution is the pivotal event that introduced the concept of people‑rule and national identity to Europe.

This chapter provides the foundational narrative of how nationalism emerged, evolved, and ultimately redefined the political map of Europe.

## The Rise of Nationalism in Europe

### What It Means
Nationalism is the idea that a group of people who share a common language, culture, or history should form a unified political entity. In Europe, the concept began to take shape during the late 18th century, especially after the French Revolution.

### How It Works
1. **French Revolutionary Ideas**  
   - The revolution introduced the notions of **_la patrie_** (the fatherland) and **_le citoyen_** (the citizen).  
   - These ideas promoted a collective identity that went beyond local or feudal loyalties.

2. **Spread of Nationalist Symbols**  
   - National symbols and publications helped spread these ideas.  
   - For example, a German almanac cover designed by journalist Andreas Rebmann in 1798 reflects early nationalist sentiment in Germany.

3. **Collective Identity Formation**  
   - By emphasizing shared citizenship and national belonging, people began to see themselves as part of a larger nation rather than merely subjects of a monarch or local lord.

### Example
- **French Revolution**: The declaration of the rights of the citizen and the promotion of the fatherland created a new sense of belonging among the French people.
- **German Almanac (1798)**: The cover designed by Andreas Rebmann illustrates how nationalist imagery was used in other European regions to foster a similar sense of collective identity.

### Important Points
- **Core Concepts**: *la patrie* (fatherland) and *le citoyen* (citizen) are central to early European nationalism.
- **Catalyst**: The French Revolution was a key catalyst for the spread of nationalist ideas across Europe.
- **Symbolic Spread**: Publications and visual symbols (like the 1798 German almanac cover) helped disseminate nationalist sentiment beyond France.
