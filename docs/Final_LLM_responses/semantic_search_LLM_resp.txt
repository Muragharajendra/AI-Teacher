**Short answer**

A nation, according to Ernst Renan, is a community of people who share a common past, a collective will, and a sense of solidarity that is continually renewed by the inhabitants themselves. Nations are important because they safeguard liberty and prevent the domination of a single law or master over all peoples.

---

## 1. What Renan sees as the attributes of a nation

| Attribute | Explanation (from Renan) |
|-----------|--------------------------|
| **Shared heroic past** | A nation is built on a long history of endeavours, sacrifice, and devotion, with great men and glorious deeds that serve as social capital. |
| **Common will** | The people must have a present‑day shared intention to act together and to pursue further achievements. |
| **Collective memory** | The nation is defined by “common glories in the past” that bind its members. |
| **Large‑scale solidarity** | It is a solidarity that extends across the whole population, not just a subset. |
| **Daily plebiscite** | The nation’s existence is continually affirmed by the inhabitants; it is a living, everyday act of consultation. |
| **Inhabitants as the province** | The people themselves are the province; each inhabitant has the right to be consulted. |
| **Non‑territorial nature** | A nation does not seek to annex or hold a country against its will; its identity is not tied to a fixed territory. |
| **Critique of other bases** | Renan rejects the idea that a nation is defined by language, race, religion, or territory. |

---

## 2. Why nations matter, in Renan’s view

1. **Guarantee of liberty** – Nations protect individual freedom by ensuring that no single law or master can dominate all peoples.
2. **Necessary for liberty** – Without distinct nations, the world would risk becoming a single, uniform authority that would erode personal and collective freedoms.
3. **Positive existence** – The very fact that nations exist is a good thing, a necessity that sustains liberty and diversity.

---

### Bottom line

A nation is a living community united by a shared past, a common will, and continual affirmation by its inhabitants. Renan argues that such communities are essential for preserving liberty and preventing the rise of a single, oppressive authority.
